# NOBODY GOES FIRST
## The cancer ward, the chip fab, and the real reason good ideas wait

ERI Labs Field Notes · June 17, 2026

---

> The arithmetic was never the holdup. The holdup was finding the first person willing to act on it before anyone else did.

---

Somewhere right now, a hospital finance committee is looking at a proposal it will probably shelve. The numbers in it are almost embarrassingly good: roughly a million and a half dollars to install computing hardware built specifically for cancer diagnostics, paid back in under three years, and several million dollars a year in savings after that, for as long as the equipment keeps running. Committees do not often get to see numbers this clean. And yet, as of this summer, almost nobody has signed the purchase order.

This isn't a story about whether the hardware works. That's a separate, testable, eventually-answerable question, and not really my subject. My subject is the gap that opens up between a decision the math has already made and a decision a human being still has to sign — a gap that can stay open for years, even when nobody disputes the math. I went looking for that gap in two industries that, on paper, have nothing to do with each other: the business of building computer chips, and the business of treating cancer with custom-made molecules. I found, in both, the identical wait. And then I found something better than a grand theory of why. I found out it had already been explained, decades ago, by people who'd never heard of either industry.

## Two clocks

Here is the cancer story, compressed. A tumor under attack from a drug or the immune system has, broadly, a menu of ways to escape. The cheapest moves — small DNA changes that don't even alter the proteins the cell makes — get tried first, for the unglamorous reason that cheap moves get tried first in biology the same way they do everywhere else. When those run out, the tumor starts making costlier, riskier changes: rewiring entire programs of gene expression, committing to a more aggressive identity. Once that commitment happens, by most of the modeling I read, it's effectively permanent.

The exact length of that overall window is something the research itself can't quite agree on — different estimates in the same body of work put it anywhere from two months to half a year, which is its own small lesson in how much confidence to assign any single number here. But the narrower clock, the one that actually determines whether a doctor can do anything about it, is more concrete: designing and deploying a precision countermeasure today — sequencing the tumor, screening a thousand candidate genetic "guides," designing a custom molecule, validating it, manufacturing it, getting it into a patient — takes, by the most detailed accounting I found, about nine weeks. Run that same process on computing hardware actually built for the shape of the problem, instead of borrowed from whatever runs today's chatbots, and it drops to about three.

Three weeks fits comfortably inside almost any version of the window above. Nine weeks is a coin flip, and on the more pessimistic estimates, already too late. Most of the difference between those two numbers, it turns out, isn't biology at all. It's computation.

## The wrong instrument

Today's AI chips are, with vanishingly few exceptions, built around one mathematical operation: multiplying big grids of numbers against each other, over and over. It's a phenomenal choice for the AI models currently running the world's chatbots, which are, underneath everything, doing exactly that kind of multiplication at enormous scale. It is a considerably worse choice for modeling how two oddly shaped molecules fit together in three-dimensional space, or for screening thousands of small genetic edits one at a time, which is closer to the actual shape of the cancer problem.

You can still solve the second kind of problem on hardware built for the first, the way you can still chop vegetables with the flat of a knife instead of the edge. It just costs you, and in this telling, what it costs is time — by some estimates, on the order of two hundred times more computer-hours than a chip actually built for the job would need, which is most of the difference between a nine-week design cycle and a three-week one. The molecule is still designable. The pattern in the data is still there. It's just buried under a few hundred times more arithmetic than the problem actually requires, on hardware that was never asked to do this job in the first place — it just happened to be the hardware that existed.

The same mismatch, it turns out, is playing out one level down, in the chip industry itself. A handful of newer chip designs, built around fundamentally different arithmetic, claim real advantages for exactly this kind of mismatched workload: lower power, faster results, in some cases cleaner guarantees that the chip computed the right answer at all, which matters enormously in anything safety-critical. None of this is controversial among the engineers who design chips for a living. What's controversial is timing, because once enough of the software world — the compilers, the libraries, the people trained on one set of tools — commits to a particular architecture, switching away from it stops being a technical choice and starts being closer to a tax. The estimates I've seen put that tipping point somewhere around forty percent of the market settling on one approach; cross it, and the cost of building for anything else starts to outweigh the benefit, regardless of which approach is actually better on the merits. Which means there's a clock running on the chip side too — roughly twenty-four months, by the same accounting, between "the better architecture is demonstrably possible" and "the worse-but-earlier one has locked in enough of the market that switching stops being rational for almost anyone."

That window closes around the same two years, 2026 to 2028, as the cancer-treatment window above. Which is a genuinely interesting coincidence. It is not, on its own, evidence of anything more than that.


## The boring, excellent explanation

The hospital that won't buy the cancer hardware isn't failing to perceive a pattern. Its finance committee has the same five-year return I opened with sitting right there in the proposal, in plain numbers. What it's actually weighing is something Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky described back in 1979, in work that eventually won a Nobel Prize: people don't judge an outcome against some neutral, absolute scale. They judge it against whatever they were expecting — last year's budget, say — and a loss against that expectation registers roughly twice as painfully as an equivalent gain feels good.

On a five-year chart, the hardware is a clear win. On a one-year chart — which is the only chart most hospital budgets actually use — year one is a million-and-a-half-dollar hole with no offsetting revenue yet, because patient volume takes time to build. Shlomo Benartzi and Richard Thaler showed in 1995 exactly why that distinction matters in practice: investors who check their retirement accounts every month find stocks far less appealing than investors who check once a year, even though they're looking at the identical investment, because the shorter the window, the more often you catch it mid-dip and feel the sting. A hospital evaluating a five-year bet on an annual cycle is doing precisely that, once a year, until the project either dies in year one or survives long enough that everyone forgets it briefly looked like a mistake.

None of this requires the committee to be confused, shortsighted, or bad at arithmetic. It requires only that the people approving the spending are evaluated annually, by people who were themselves evaluated annually, going back as far as anyone wants to trace it. Which is to say: it requires the committee to be staffed by humans.

## Why nobody jumps first

There's a second, independent reason the wait persists, and it has nothing to do with anyone's appetite for risk. It's a coordination problem, and coordination problems don't care how good the underlying technology is.

Picture every hospital, and every chip company, privately agreeing the new approach is better — and every one of them still waiting, because the value of moving depends on how many others have already moved. Go first alone, and you absorb the entire cost of working out the kinks yourself, with no peer group to compare notes with and no trained workforce to hire from. Go once enough others have already gone, and the cost drops by an order of magnitude, because somebody else already found the mistakes. Economists have a precise term for markets that can settle into either of two stable states — everyone stays put, or everyone switches — depending on small, early, almost accidental events rather than which option is actually better. Brian Arthur laid out the mechanism in 1989, using the keyboard layout under your fingers as the textbook case: it didn't win because it's a good way to type. It won because enough typewriters shipped with it early enough that un-shipping it got more expensive every year afterward, for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's fingers. Thomas Schelling and Mark Granovetter, working on entirely unrelated questions in the 1970s — how neighborhoods end up segregated, how riots start — both landed on the same underlying mathematics: every actor has a personal threshold, a share of everyone else who needs to have already moved before moving stops being a bad idea for them personally. Below that threshold, staying put is the rational choice for every single person, even while everyone privately agrees the group would be better off moving together. Cross it, and the whole thing can flip within a season.

That's the real content behind a phrase like "forty percent market lock-in." It isn't a law of physics. It's the point at which enough chip designers and hospital systems have already committed that the holdouts' math flips — and below that point, every one of them is being perfectly, individually rational by waiting for somebody else to go first.

It's also worth saying, in fairness to the people doing the waiting, that not all of this is timidity. Robert McDonald and Daniel Siegel, and later Avinash Dixit and Robert Pindyck in a book that became standard reading for anyone making large irreversible bets, showed that the option to wait for more information carries real, calculable value of its own — value a simple five-year-return spreadsheet skips entirely, because it implicitly treats "invest now" and "never invest" as the only two choices on the table. A hospital that wants to see whether a first-generation vendor actually ships working hardware, whether a biomarker actually validates in someone else's patients, and whether the insurance codes actually materialize, before committing its own money, isn't necessarily being a coward. It may simply be pricing, correctly, the real chance that going first is the more expensive mistake. The honest answer to that kind of hesitation isn't bravado. It's making the open questions resolve faster and more publicly — specific, dated, checkable predictions, scored honestly whether they land or miss — so that waiting gets less comfortable on a schedule, instead of everyone waiting indefinitely for a certainty that was never going to show up on its own.

## Whose name is on the memo

There's a last piece, and it's the most human one. David Scharfstein and Jeremy Stein modeled it in 1990: the people making these calls are rarely spending their own money, and they're judged with a strange asymmetry. A hospital executive who champions the new hardware and it underperforms owns that outcome personally — their proposal, their budget line, their name on the memo. An executive who sticks with the familiar approach while the whole field quietly falls behind owns almost nothing, because "the entire industry was slow on this" doesn't end careers the way "I spent a million and a half on the thing that didn't work" can. That asymmetry will push a perfectly rational, perfectly well-informed person toward the safe, familiar choice even while they privately believe the alternative has the better odds — for reasons that have nothing to do with chromatin or chip architecture and everything to do with how performance reviews work.

This isn't a fringe idea dressed up after the fact, either. Last October, the Nobel Prize in Economics went to Joel Mokyr, working alone, and to Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt, sharing the other half, for working out — separately and then together — how innovation actually displaces the old way of doing things. Mokyr's share was for identifying what societies need beyond the bare fact that a new method works: a real understanding of *why* it works, which is what lets the improvement spread instead of staying a curiosity. Aghion and Howitt's share was for turning Joseph Schumpeter's old idea of "creative destruction" into something mathematically precise — newer products continuously entering markets and displacing the companies selling the older ones, which is also, not incidentally, how sustained economic growth actually happens. The Academy's own statement didn't stop at celebrating displacement. It noted that growth can't be taken for granted, because the people who'd be disadvantaged by an innovation have every rational incentive to slow it down. A chip company with a comfortable, profitable line of business built on the old architecture has precisely the hospital executive's problem: the better substrate doesn't just cost money to adopt, it cannibalizes a working, attributable, career-safe line of revenue in exchange for an unproven one. Seen this way, the holdouts aren't failing to notice the pattern. They're correctly identifying who personally pays for it.

## What would actually move it

None of the things slowing this down — the annual-budget framing, the coordination trap, the legitimate value of waiting, the career-risk asymmetry, and the pull of a story more exciting than the truth — get solved by a sharper pitch deck, a more confident chart, or, for that matter, a tidy constant borrowed from geometry. Each one has a known fix, and almost none of those fixes require proving the underlying technology works any harder than it's already proven itself.

You re-time the cash flow instead of re-arguing the decision: lease the hardware rather than buy it outright, so the year-one hit never shows up as a lump sum against this year's budget at all. You manufacture the critical mass on purpose instead of waiting for it to occur naturally: ten or fifteen institutions committing at the same moment, so no single one has to be the lone early mover. You shrink the value of waiting by resolving the genuine uncertainties on a public schedule — specific, dated, falsifiable claims, scored honestly when they miss as well as when they land — so that "let's wait and see" gets measurably less comfortable with each one that resolves. You rename the status quo as the active choice it actually is, with its own ongoing cost, instead of letting it hide as the silent default nobody has to defend. And you diffuse the personal risk by making the move collective and simultaneous, so that no single name is the only one on the memo if it doesn't work out.

None of that requires a unifying law of the universe. It requires recognizing that the holdup was never about visibility — the numbers were sitting in the proposal the whole time, in plain ink, for anyone willing to read them. It was about who has to be the first person to act on a number everyone else can also see perfectly well, and what it costs that person, personally, if they turn out to be wrong before anyone else does.

That hospital proposal from the opening isn't waiting on a sharper instrument to reveal some hidden pattern. It's waiting on someone to make going first not feel like the most expensive thing in the room. That's a financing problem, a coordination problem, and a management problem — and all three have been solved before, by people with tools considerably more reliable than a number that simply happens to rhyme.
